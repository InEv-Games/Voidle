War had been a means for organisms for millennia. 'Cells eating cells' is how simple the equation is. Similarly, humanity's nearest living relatives still cross into one another's territory to kill, and the line that became human carried the habit for millions of years before it had a word for it. Its use hardly changed. A society is a system held in balance by a great many small forces: kinship, trade, habit, fear. War was the large one, the force you applied when you wanted the balance to settle somewhere else. For modern humans the thing being moved was 'society' itself, and the 'great powers' moved its balance wherever they wanted it, for one reason or another. To topple governments. To make peace for their 'kind'. To make conditions at the bottom of the balance so hard that the bottom thinned, and the Earth's population with it, so that their own legacy could prosper better, and for longer. It served them well enough, up until their end days.

Late in the twenty-first century, in what was already being called the Age of Artificial Intelligence, the old societies collapsed, and the people left in the wreckage turned out to be 'undivertable'. No pressure moved them where it was meant to. So, as a last resort, some of the great powers 'pushed the big red button'.

As the nuclear war began, a long-planned project was also set into motion: Project Lazarus. Its signatories made an odd table. There were some governments, some families whose names had never appeared in print, and some social constructs that had existed only in the Earth's last ten years. They shook hands on one thing. If the worst ever came true, humanity's frozen embryos would be sent to LHS 1140 b, a rocky world circling a dim red star in Cetus, forty-eight light-years away, so that humanity could be restored.

The great ships were designed all over the world at once, and all of them were the same kind of thing: seedships, the machines futurists had been sketching for a century under the name of embryo space colonisation. The embryos travelled under vitrification, held at −196 °C in liquid nitrogen, not so much frozen as turned to glass. The cryobank was kept in triplicate across separate sections of the hull, so that no single breach could take them all. Beside it lay the birthing deck, rows of artificial wombs for ectogenesis, dark until there was a world to wake for. Forward stood the Whipple shields, thin bumpers set apart from the wall they guarded, so that whatever struck the first would burst into a spreading cloud the next could catch. And astern was the drive: fusion pulse propulsion of the Project Daedalus lineage. Pellets of deuterium and helium-3 burnt two hundred and fifty times a second inside a magnetic nozzle, driving the whole mass up to a tenth of the speed of light. At that speed, forty-eight light-years is a crossing of about four hundred and eighty years. Hence, the ships would be run by minds that had been built rather than born. On the night they left, they hung in the starry sky like charms, small and bright, the only stars that moved.

Alas, humanity ceased to exist. The citizens of the one and only great ship to ever reach 'a habitable planet' called this incident: The Catastrophe.

Eight great ships left the Earth, and six of them suffered the same engine failure in the same reach of the dark, about twenty light-years out. A seventh, Aniara, met something out there for which there is still no better name than a cosmic entity. Their corpus is still in debate with it to this day, and will be forever.

I-330 was the lucky one. Caught in a rain of asteroids, her Whipple shields taking the strikes, she turned off her route and caught a wormhole. The log for those seconds holds one entry: 'Quantum dimension rippled.' When the ship came through, she was at an exoplanet just like LHS 1140 b.
